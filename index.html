GRAY PAPERS / 01 — PRACTICAL AUTOMATION

AI Agents, Same Old Bureaucracy

AI agents can make individual tasks dramatically faster while making the overall system more expensive. DevOps learned this lesson decades ago: optimize the constraint, not the activity.

THE SHORT VERSION

Faster agents don’t automatically make a faster business. Find the bottleneck. Improve it. Then look again.

Somehow, in our rush to replace corporate bureaucracy with AI, we have begun rebuilding corporate bureaucracy—with AI.

I spent a large part of my career in DevOps. Across hundreds of pipeline implementations and enterprise transformations, the same problem kept turning up wearing different clothes: a system wasn’t slow because every part was slow. Something was constraining the flow of work.

Find it. Fix it. Look again, because the bottleneck probably moved. That’s an oversimplification of DevOps, obviously. But it’s a surprisingly useful lens for the agent boom.

01 / We’ve seen this movie before

Lean manufacturing and the Theory of Constraints gave us an uncomfortable truth: the output of a system is limited by its constraint.

If one factory station can finish ten widgets an hour, making the station before it produce a thousand doesn’t give you a thousand finished widgets. It gives you ten, plus a very impressive pile.

Software has inventory, too. It just looks like pull requests waiting for review, tickets waiting for QA, and projects waiting for someone to make a decision.

The objective was never to make developers type faster. It was to improve the flow of value from an idea to a customer.

We just gave developers machines that type very, very fast. The distinction matters.

Productivity
How much work an agent produces.
Throughput
How much finished work the system delivers.
Value
Whether that work makes anything better for the business or its customers.

Those are three different measurements. Optimizing one can make the others worse.

02 / The $5,000 meeting about a blue button

Enterprise processes accumulate. An incident creates an approval. An audit creates a control. A VP creates a committee. Another department wants representation. Eventually, there is a meeting before the meeting.

Every addition can have a reasonable explanation. The resulting system can still be completely insane.

I’ve watched rooms full of expensive people adjudicate decisions far beneath the level where their judgment created value. Does senior IT leadership really need to decide whether a button meets the corporate color palette? Apparently, sometimes. I’ve seen it.

Now replace those people with a developer agent, a product agent, an architecture agent, a security agent, and a manager agent coordinating the other agents.

The diagram looks wonderful. The execution trace is less flattering: build, reject, rewrite, object, rewrite again. We’ve automated the org chart—including some of its worst habits. And the meter is running through every conversation.

That doesn’t make multiple agents a bad idea. In its research-system write-up, Anthropic reported roughly 15 times the token use of chat interactions, alongside strong results on suitable research tasks. The economic question is whether the extra value justifies the extra work.

03 / Congratulations. The bottleneck moved.

Imagine development takes five days and review takes one. An extraordinary coding agent cuts development to twenty minutes.

AN ILLUSTRATIVE DELIVERY QUEUE

Before
5 days to build
1 day to review
After
20 minutes to build
1 day to review

Development got dramatically faster. Review is now the constraint. Keep accelerating development and you grow the queue.

This isn’t AI failing. It’s what successful optimization does: it exposes the next constraint.

DORA’s 2025 research describes AI as an amplifier of an organization’s existing strengths and weaknesses. My takeaway: a faster generator doesn’t repair a slow delivery system. It makes that system harder to ignore.

04 / Cheap work still needs a reason to exist

Once work becomes cheap, we start producing things we wouldn’t previously have bothered producing. Another prototype. Another dashboard. A 40-page specification. Five alternate implementations of the same feature.

Technically, productivity is exploding. But every artifact can create a new obligation: someone has to read it, check it, integrate it, or maintain it.

“We can generate it” is a capability.
“Someone needs it” is a reason.

Before commissioning more work, ask what decision it will improve or what customer problem it will solve. A cheaper artifact is still expensive if it consumes the scarcest thing in the system: attention.

05 / Move the rules before the rework

Security, architecture, and compliance matter. Controls exist because organizations have real risks. The useful question is whether each control’s value exceeds the friction it adds—and whether it arrives in time to help.

If a security agent already knows an authentication pattern is prohibited, why let the development agent discover that after building the application?

  1. Before executionPut security rules, architecture standards, and acceptance criteria where the builder can use them.
  2. During executionAutomate deterministic checks. Enforce permissions through the platform.
  3. By exceptionBring in human judgment where the risk, ambiguity, or tradeoff actually needs it.

DevOps people have a name for this: shift left. Catch the problem while it’s cheap to fix. Governance becomes more useful when it prevents rework instead of waiting at the end to request it.

06 / Count the outcome. Include the bill.

Tokens tell you about consumption. Tasks tell you about activity. Neither tells you whether something useful reached a customer.

A MORE USEFUL UNIT

Cost per accepted outcome

Models + tools + infrastructure
+ review + reworkAccepted business outcomes

Include human and agent review. Count results the business can actually use.

Then ask a few less glamorous, much more revealing questions:

  • How long did intent-to-production take?
  • How much work survived the first review?
  • How many review and rework cycles did it need?
  • Did the accepted result improve something the business cares about?

Once token usage becomes the KPI, we’ll optimize token usage. We’ll have reinvented measuring programmers by lines of code, with a much more exciting invoice.

07 / Find the next scarce thing

I’m wildly optimistic about AI agents. That makes disciplined systems thinking more useful, not less.

When production gets cheap, something else stays scarce: review, judgment, integration, trust, customer attention. Our job is to find it.

TRY THIS WITH ONE WORKFLOW

Follow one piece of work from request to customer. Find where it waits longest. Improve that handoff before adding another agent.

Value reaching the customer is the objective. Fix what gets in its way. Then look again.

The constraint will have moved. We’ve done this before.

GRAY PAPERS / 02 — COMMUNICATION & AI

Communication is the New Programming Language

Clear thinking, domain experience, and the ability to explain what matters are becoming the most useful tools in the builder’s kit.

Working draft / a perspective in progress

THE SHORT VERSION

You don’t have to know every programming language to help build a useful system. You do need to explain the outcome, the constraints, and what “working” looks like.

For a long time, the first question about building software was a language question. Java? C#? Python? Before an idea could become useful, someone had to translate it into instructions a computer would accept.

AI is changing where that translation happens. You can increasingly describe an outcome, work through the details in conversation, and get help producing an implementation.

For the business owner, the first question is becoming much more interesting: can you explain what you actually need?

01 / Syntax is less of a gate. Judgment still matters.

Most operators don’t care whether the system getting their quotes out on time uses Java or Python. They care that the quote is accurate, the customer receives it, and someone follows up.

Engineering still matters: security, reliability, testing, and maintenance don’t disappear because a conversation produced the code. Someone has to judge whether the result works.

Communication is becoming a way to build. The quality of what you build depends on the quality of what you can explain.

The scarce skill moves upstream: define the problem, name the constraints, explain the exceptions, and recognize a convincing answer that has missed the point.

02 / A beautiful sentence can be a terrible brief

“Build me a better sales process” is grammatically clear. Operationally, it leaves almost everything unresolved.

Better for whom? Where does a lead arrive? Who owns the next step? What counts as qualified? When should the system stop and ask a person?

Effective communication makes those decisions visible. It separates what must happen from what would be nice. It supplies examples and says what failure looks like.

ONE SENTENCE WORTH BORROWING

“Before you build anything, tell me what you think I mean.”

A misunderstanding is cheaper to fix in a conversation than in a system your team already relies on.

03 / Your experience is part of the specification

A quote can be technically complete and commercially useless. An appointment can be booked and still be impossible to service. A record can exist in the CRM and still be wrong.

People who work inside a business know the details that rarely make it into a process diagram. That knowledge is an advantage—once you can articulate it.

TAKE SCHEDULING

The easy request

“Find the next available slot.”

The dispatcher’s version

“Find a slot with enough travel time, a qualified technician, the right equipment, and a customer who can actually let us in.”

Same calendar. Very different problem.

Those details give the AI a more useful problem to solve. They also give you a way to check its answer.

04 / Give context, then question the answer

We explain things differently to a beginner and an experienced colleague. The vocabulary, examples, and assumptions change because effective communication depends on context.

AI conversations benefit from the same care. Describe the real operating environment. Name the difficult tradeoffs. Explain the useful shorthand. The goal is shared understanding, not an impressive collection of acronyms.

An expert tone is not evidence. AI can follow a mistaken premise with remarkable confidence. Ask it to surface assumptions, examine alternatives, and show how you could verify the result against real examples.

05 / Build the brief before the system

You don’t need a novel. A few paragraphs can give a builder the decisions that matter.

  1. The outcomeWhat should get better, and for whom?
  2. The work todayWhere does it start, who handles it, and where does it stall?
  3. The boundariesWhat must happen? What must never happen? When should a person step in?
  4. The examplesShow an ordinary case and an exception.
  5. The proofHow will you check that the change actually helped?

THE BRIEF, IN PRACTICE

“Reduce the time our dispatcher spends assigning routine jobs. Suggest slots only when the technician has the right qualifications and enough travel time. Send uncertain cases to the dispatcher. Test suggestions against last week’s actual jobs before letting the system book anything.”

That is already a meaningful piece of building. It turns ambition into decisions that can be implemented, challenged, and measured.

06 / The new builder knows what matters

Operators, dispatchers, sales leaders, and specialists carry knowledge that can now take a shorter path into a working system.

Clear communication supplies direction. Domain experience supplies judgment. Engineering turns the result into something people can depend on.

TRY IT ON ONE RECURRING HEADACHE

Pick a task your team knows too well. Write the brief above. Ask the AI to explain it back and identify what’s missing.

Explain. Inspect. Correct. Test. That’s a useful place to start building.

GRAY PAPERS / 03 — AI & SMALL BUSINESS

AI Changes Your Business Before You Use It

Customers can research more, compare faster, and arrive with different expectations. Small businesses need a clear account of their value and the processes to deliver it.

THE SHORT VERSION

Your business can choose when to adopt AI. Your customers and competitors make their own choices. Understand what those choices change, make your value visible, and improve the work that helps you deliver it.

A small business can decide that AI isn’t a priority this year. There may be good reasons. The team is busy, the tools are confusing, and replacing a process that mostly works has a cost.

The market doesn’t wait for that decision.

Customers can use AI to research a purchase, examine a quote, compare alternatives, and prepare questions before they ever speak to you. Competitors can use it to respond sooner and keep opportunities from getting lost between systems.

That changes the environment in which your business operates, even if nobody on your team opens an AI tool.

01 / Your customer has done some homework

A customer who arrives with research has a different conversation with you.

They may know the manufacturer’s suggested price for a piece of equipment. They may have compared specifications or asked an AI to identify alternatives. They can arrive with more information, in less time, than a customer might have gathered on their own a few years ago.

That doesn’t make your expertise less valuable. It changes how you need to explain it.

The equipment is one part of the job

Take an HVAC contractor. A customer finds the equipment online and asks why the installed price is higher than the hardware’s MSRP.

That is a reasonable question.

The answer might include installation, configuration, compatibility with an existing system, knowledge of local conditions, warranty handling, and someone to call when something goes wrong. Those things have value. The customer needs to understand which of them they are buying.

If the quote presents one unexplained number, it leaves the customer to make the comparison with whatever information they can find.

Make the service visible before asking someone to pay for it.

02 / Give “better service” something concrete to mean

Small businesses often have real advantages: experience with local conditions, long relationships, practical knowledge, and people who can make decisions without passing the customer through several departments.

Those advantages are easy to describe vaguely.

“We offer better service” could mean almost anything. A customer needs to know what will happen.

Who helps them choose? Who handles installation? What happens when something goes wrong? Which problems are covered, and which require another conversation?

Explain the work. Then deliver it.

A useful quote or proposal makes the scope understandable:

  • What the customer receives.
  • What work is required to make it useful.
  • What support continues after delivery.
  • What the customer is responsible for.
  • Where the limits and exceptions are.

The same clarity matters after the sale. If responsiveness is part of your value, someone needs to own the response. If local expertise matters, it should show up in the recommendation.

AI can help prepare explanations and keep records consistent. The business still has to supply the expertise and fulfill the promise.

03 / Close the loop

Customers are used to services that work with very little effort on their part. An app updates in the background. A package arrives quickly. They can see where an order stands without calling someone to ask.

A small business doesn’t need Amazon’s infrastructure to serve its customers well. It does need to understand the expectations those experiences create.

A fast acknowledgment is useful. It becomes much less useful when nothing follows it.

A customer submits an inquiry. Someone replies. A quote gets promised. Then the request sits in an inbox while everyone assumes someone else is handling it.

The customer experiences the entire sequence. They don’t see the internal handoffs, and they shouldn’t need to chase them.

A reply creates a next step

Closing the loop means that a request reaches a clear outcome. It may be a completed job, an accepted quote, a useful answer, or an honest explanation that you cannot help.

Along the way, the customer should understand what happens next.

AN ORDINARY SERVICE REQUEST

  1. The request arrives and is acknowledged.
  2. Someone owns it.
  3. Missing information is collected.
  4. The business completes the work or explains the next step.
  5. The customer receives confirmation.

Automation can help with intake, routing, reminders, and status updates. A strong relationship can also make the process work: a person knows the customer, remembers the situation, and follows through.

Many businesses will use both. The test is whether the customer gets an answer and the commitment gets fulfilled.

04 / Start with the work everyone keeps repeating

A business does not need to put an AI chatbot in front of its customers to benefit from AI. The back office is a useful place to begin because so much of its work involves carrying information from one place to another.

Someone takes notes. Someone rewrites the notes. Someone explains the meeting to a colleague who missed it. A week later, someone searches a message thread to find out what was decided.

Each step may be small. Together, they consume time that could go toward customers, decisions, or work that requires experience.

Give the meeting a useful afterlife

Consider a recurring team meeting.

With appropriate recording and access rules, a system can transcribe the discussion, prepare a summary, identify proposed action items, and store the record somewhere the team can find it. People who were absent can catch up without asking everyone else to repeat the meeting.

A person should check the decisions, owners, and deadlines before the summary becomes the working record. A suggestion made halfway through a discussion should not quietly turn into an approved commitment.

The value comes from what the team can do afterward: retrieve the decision, understand the context, and follow up.

Start with a repetitive task whose result is easy to inspect. Learn whether the system saves useful time after review and correction are included. Some tasks need AI to interpret messy information; others can be handled with a simple rule or the software you already own.

05 / Decide what the system is allowed to do

“Use AI” leaves an important question unanswered: how much authority are you giving it?

A system that drafts a customer reply has a different role from one that sends the reply. A system that suggests an appointment has a different role from one that commits a technician to a job.

The information may be similar. The consequences are different.

Put the boundary beside the task

For each workflow, decide:

  • What information the system can access.
  • What it can prepare or recommend.
  • What it can change without asking.
  • Which decisions need a person.
  • What happens when information is incomplete or conflicting.

A meeting summary can propose follow-up items. A manager can confirm who owns them. A scheduling system can suggest an opening. A dispatcher can handle a job with unusual equipment or access requirements.

People with domain experience know why the ordinary process sometimes fails. That knowledge belongs in the design, including the point at which the system should stop and ask.

06 / Cloud or local? Follow the information and the responsibility.

The choice between a cloud service and a local system deserves more attention than a quick preference for convenience or control.

A cloud service can reduce the work of operating the technology. It also introduces a provider whose terms, access controls, retention practices, and business decisions matter.

A local system can give you more direct control over where information is processed. It also gives you responsibility for maintaining, securing, backing up, and supporting that system.

Neither arrangement removes the need to understand the workflow.

Ask questions that survive the sales demo

What information will enter the system? Who can access it? How long is it retained? Can it be used for other purposes?

Who controls the account and the records? Can you export them? What happens if you change providers, the service becomes unavailable, or the person who configured everything leaves?

Then consider authority again. Reading approved reference material is one level of access. Sending messages, changing customer records, or making commitments on the company’s behalf is another.

Choose the setup against the sensitivity of the information, the consequences of a mistake, and your ability to operate it responsibly. Outsourcing a system does not mean outsourcing the decision about what risk your business is comfortable accepting.

07 / Pick one promise and make it easier to keep

You don’t need to settle every question about AI before improving one part of the business.

Choose a promise your customers care about. Perhaps quotes arrive when expected. Perhaps service requests get an answer. Perhaps customers can understand exactly what they are paying for.

Follow the work behind that promise. Find the repeated effort, the unclear ownership, and the places where information disappears.

Then make a limited change and inspect the result.

TRY THIS WITH ONE WORKFLOW

Choose a recurring customer request. Write down what the customer expects, who owns each step, and what counts as finished. Identify one repetitive task that could be automated and one decision that should stay with a person.

After testing the change, check whether work finishes sooner, mistakes decrease, and customers need to follow up less. Include the cost of the tools and the time spent reviewing their work.

AI is changing how customers evaluate businesses and what they expect from them. Small businesses still have valuable things to offer: judgment, relationships, regional knowledge, and responsibility for the result.

Make those things easy to understand. Build the processes that let you deliver them consistently.
